Preserve Sunnyside Environmental School’s K–8 Environmental Education Program

A coalition letter to the Portland Public Schools Board of Education and Superintendent Dr. Kimberlee Armstrong.

0 organizations and individuals have signed. See who’s signed

Dear Portland Public Schools Board of Education and Superintendent Dr. Kimberlee Armstrong,

We, the undersigned environmental organizations, community partners, and community leaders, urge Portland Public Schools to preserve Sunnyside Environmental School as a K–8 environmental education program.

For 32 years, Sunnyside Environmental School has educated students, built community partnerships, and developed generations of environmental stewards and advocates. As organizations and leaders committed to environmental protection, climate action, and environmental justice, we recognize Sunnyside as a vital public resource—not only for its students and families, but for the city of Portland and the entire state of Oregon.

Removing Sunnyside’s middle grades does not preserve its environmental education program. It dismantles it.

Sunnyside’s environmental education model is an intentionally designed, nine-year curriculum developed by educators and the school community over decades. Beginning in kindergarten, students build connections to the natural world through hands-on, place-based learning. As they grow, their understanding expands beyond their schoolyard and neighborhood to Oregon’s rivers, forests, mountains, and coastal ecosystems.

The middle grades are not simply an extension of this curriculum. They are its culmination.

It is during these critical years that students begin transforming their knowledge into meaningful action, developing the confidence, skills, and sense of responsibility to become leaders in their communities. Removing grades 6–8 eliminates the very experiences that the earlier years are designed to prepare students for.

Preserving an environmental focus in grades K–5 is not the same as preserving Sunnyside’s K–8 environmental education model.

Sunnyside exceeds the district’s benchmarks for enrollment, attendance, academic achievement, staff retention, and principal stability. It does so while serving twice the proportion of students with IEPs or 504 plans that the state funding formula accounts for. Many of these students and their families have worked hard to find an educational environment where they feel supported, included, and able to thrive. For these students, Sunnyside is not simply another school option. It is a community where they have found belonging and success.

Sunnyside’s middle school students participate in weekly field studies, working alongside community partners to restore habitats, protect waterways, remove invasive species, and investigate environmental challenges affecting their communities. They also learn to engage in civic processes, advocate for environmental protections, and understand their responsibility to the people and places around them.

This year, Sunnyside middle school students discovered a two-mile fish kill in Johnson Creek during a field study at Tideman Johnson Park. Their report helped prompt an Oregon Department of Environmental Quality investigation that identified an industrial chemical release from Precision Castparts Corp. as the cause.

This is environmental education in action. These students are not simply learning about environmental problems. They are developing the knowledge, awareness, and agency to identify problems, speak up, and contribute to solutions.

Sunnyside’s approach cultivates active hope at a time when young people are increasingly aware of the environmental challenges facing their generation. Rather than leaving students feeling powerless in the face of climate change, the program gives them opportunities to make a tangible difference.

At a time when Oregon is experiencing increasingly severe wildfires, extreme heat, declining snowpack, and threats to ecosystems and water resources, we should be expanding access to this kind of education—not dismantling an established model that has spent decades developing it.

The proposed restructuring also raises serious concerns about educational equity and access. Sunnyside has created a learning environment where many neurodivergent students and students receiving special education services have found belonging and success. Its experiential, interdisciplinary approach offers multiple pathways for students to engage, learn, and demonstrate their strengths.

The district must carefully consider what would be lost for these students and families if the program were divided, particularly for those who have already experienced challenges finding an educational environment that meets their needs.

We believe educational equity should mean expanding access to successful, inclusive learning models across Portland Public Schools, not eliminating opportunities that are already working for students.

Sunnyside’s impact extends well beyond its classrooms. Its alumni have gone on to become environmental lawyers, engineers, community organizers, and advocates. Its students have participated in efforts to protect Oregon’s forests, wildlife, and waterways, demonstrating what becomes possible when young people are given the tools and encouragement to participate in civic life.

Portland and Oregon need more schools that cultivate this kind of environmental literacy, community engagement, and student leadership.

For environmental organizations across Oregon, protecting Sunnyside Environmental School is about more than preserving one school. Our work depends on communities that understand the importance of environmental stewardship, recognize the connections between environmental health and social justice, and are empowered to take meaningful action.

Watershed councils, conservation organizations, climate advocates, and community partners work every day to protect the natural resources and ecosystems that sustain our communities. Sunnyside is preparing the next generation to carry that work forward.

Its students are not only learning about the environmental challenges we face; they are becoming the informed, engaged, and compassionate leaders our communities will need to address them. Dismantling this program would be a loss not only for Sunnyside families, but for Oregon’s broader environmental movement and the future of environmental advocacy in our state.

As environmental organizations and community leaders, we recognize that addressing the climate crisis requires more than policies and commitments. It requires educating and empowering the next generation to understand complex environmental challenges and take meaningful action.

Portland Public Schools has made commitments to climate justice and sustainability. Preserving Sunnyside’s complete K–8 environmental education program is an opportunity for the district to demonstrate those commitments through action.

We call on the Portland Public Schools Board of Education to reject the recommendation to remove grades 6–8 from Sunnyside Environmental School and preserve its full K–8 environmental education program.

We also urge the district to recognize Sunnyside as a model worth protecting, investing in, and learning from as PPS works to expand equitable access to meaningful environmental education throughout the district.

The students of Sunnyside have spent years learning that their voices matter, that communities can create change, and that standing up for the environment is both a responsibility and a possibility.

Now, those same students are standing up for the school that taught them these lessons.

We ask Portland Public Schools to listen to them.

Sincerely,
The Undersigned Environmental Organizations, Community Partners, and Community Leaders
