Sunnyside Environmental School Community Statement in Response to the Superintendent’s Final “Rightsizing” Recommendation

Our community wholeheartedly opposes Portland Public Schools Superintendent Dr. Kimberlee Armstrong’s final “rightsizing” recommendation for Sunnyside Environmental School. The Superintendent’s final recommendation converts Sunnyside from a K-8 to a K-5. Our middle schoolers would move to Hosford, and the middle-grades environmental program that has defined our school for 32 years would end. The district lists this as a change “in response to community feedback,” noting that Sunnyside “remains an environmental focus school.” That framing fits a pattern across the plan: K-8 conversions and program consolidations aren’t counted as closures, which hides how many students are actually losing their school. For our middle schoolers and their families, this is an effective closure of their school and their program is being taken away.

This isn’t what our community asked for. We have repeatedly shared with district officials and the board that our program and our school are defined by our nine-year curriculum. We provided this feedback in our community letter now signed by 3,751 people. Our alumni said it in a letter signed by 549 former students. We said it in testimony at board meetings, directly to the Superintendent at her visit to Sunnyside, and in one-on-one meetings with school board directors and with district leadership. Retaining the focus option in the elementary grades may sound on the surface like a compromise, but our curriculum was developed and is held by our teachers, and this plan offers no guarantee that they’ll be able to remain at Sunnyside.

The plan dismantles our K-8 environmental education program. Two-thirds of a program is not a program. Our curriculum is an intentionally scaffolded nine-year arc that draws on the work of renowned place-based educator David Sobel and his model of Concentric Circles of Community. Following that developmental framework, our kindergarten through second-grade students begin in the innermost circle, focusing on building empathy and falling in love with the outdoors, with environmental contexts of home, family, and their neighborhoods. As these circles expand in third through fifth grade, children transition into active exploration and background knowledge-building around local Pacific Northwest ecosystems. Finally, in middle school, our young adolescents reach the outermost circle: social action. As their prefrontal cortices develop and these adolescents begin noticing hypocrisy and injustice in the world, we channel that energy into tangible civic engagement.

This deliberate progression is research-based, and engaging with local issues and getting results is what leads our middle schoolers towards agency. For example, they helped save the Elliott State Forest from privatization and logging in 2017. It’s how they worked to get the Southern Resident Orca populations on the endangered species list in 2024. It’s how this year they recently discovered and reported a toxic waste spill and fish die-off in Southeast Portland’s Johnson Creek. These are just a few examples of our middle-school students’ efforts.

We don’t drop environmental catastrophe into the laps of five-year-olds. Instead, we build empathy and a profound love of place first, so that by middle school, students are ready to fight for it. As one of our fifth-grade teachers described it: Our K-5 years are the ladder you climb to reach the high dive, and the middle grades are the pool. Without the pool, you climb all that way and there’s nothing to jump into.

Ending the only public environmental justice K-8 in the state would be a failure of vision—we can’t lose this generation of changemakers. Our alumni make plain what is being lost: “Ending a program that has decades of success in building agency, fostering a love of learning, and increasing civic engagement at a time when we are experiencing the impact of climate and environmental destruction makes no sense.” This plan directly undermines PPS’s own aims “to educate and empower students as leaders in the just transition to a sustainable city and restorative society.” It also flies in the face of repeated claims by district, city, and state leaders who believe that more climate justice education is needed within our schools, not less.

Neurodivergent students and those with disabilities will be hit hardest. Sunnyside is a place of last refuge for many students who have struggled in other PPS settings, and it is one of the only suitable options for those students in the middle school years. Ending K-8s is not evidence based1, harms students who need different learning environments, and is not equitable for students receiving special education services2, including neurodivergent students who are not on IEPs. The proposed changes dismantle a successful program that serves many of these students in a cost effective way. A disproportionately high share of Sunnyside’s lottery middle school students are on IEPs (37%, school provided-data), reflecting the fact that parents are specifically seeking out suitable school climates for their children.

Many Sunnyside parents now credibly fear their children will be placed in more restrictive settings: the Receiving School Change Recommendation for Sunnyside states, “[f]or the Sunnyside 6th-8th graders attending Hosford, Hosford will add a CB classroom to address neurodivergent Special Education needs.” A new self-contained classroom is not the answer we asked for, nor is it the least restrictive environment for students who are currently successful in general education classrooms. Sunnyside does not have a single student whose current placement is a focus classroom, so any migration of these students to a more restrictive environment would be a gross disregard of their Free Appropriate Public Education (FAPE) rights.

Further, Sunnyside’s culture, pedagogical model and approach to inclusivity provide a psychologically safe environment for these students, one that cannot be replicated through a restrictive CB classroom.

It will cost the district more than it saves to dismantle Sunnyside’s cost-effective model. Creating a new CB classroom at Hosford would come at great financial cost, whereas Sunnyside operates a K-8 Learning Center model at below the district middle school average ($10,462 per student vs. $10,904 district middle school average in 2026–27), despite serving a high proportion (28%) of SpED students in the middle grades. Adding a CB classroom at Hosford would require one teacher FTE and three paraeducators at a minimum of cost of $128,4263. Sunnyside’s staffing subsidy for grades 6-8 is 0.4FTE ($64,000). Our per-pupil cost is in the bottom quarter of all PPS elementary, K-8 and middle schools.

There are lower-impact ways to meet the district’s goals for inner Southeast. PPS’s final plan addresses the challenge of too many K-5 buildings in inner SE, aging facilities, and the need for stable staffing and resources. We have invited the district to consider a collaborative plan in inner SE to meet the same goals another way, namely, keeping the Buckman community together, and allowing Sunnyside to remain a hybrid neighborhood/focus option K-8. We have worked with an enrollment forecasting expert who is a member of our parent community to show that the math works and that there are multiple solutions that could be explored. Such solutions could provide win-win outcomes for PPS, including leaving two buildings fully or nearly fully enrolled through 2031–32, instead of one severely under-enrolled; less disruption for the Buckman community, which is already losing its building and program, and 180 fewer middle school students losing their school and their program. Alternative solutions would also reduce impact on Sunnyside seventh graders, a cohort that, under the current plan, will attend four different school buildings in four years (Sunnyside → Hosford → Marshall → Cleveland). We continue to believe that alternative positive solutions in inner SE are possible and welcome any opportunity for collaborative problem solving.

Across PPS, this plan creates more problems than it solves. We understand that the district faces real challenges and needs real solutions. This plan does not provide them. The plan does not solve PPS’s pressing budget crisis and its persistent teaching and learning problems. It may, in fact, exacerbate them, and accelerate out-migration from the district for families that feel that “access to more stable schools may not be worth the cost of community disruption” (SEGC audit, p. 46), causing added enrollment declines and deeper financial losses. It tears communities apart and a rushed, poorly implemented transition will cause lasting harm and instability. Our schools deserve a plan for investment and growth before closures and cuts are considered.

We stand with other school communities in opposing the final recommendation. We ask the board, again, to reject the proposed dismantling of Sunnyside, and keep Sunnyside Environmental School a K-8 with its environmental focus program intact.

Notes

  1. PPS’s own OSAS comparison shows no advantage for 6-8 schools over K-8s, and its data presented to the Teaching and Learning Committee shows the highest-performing middle schoolers with IEPs attend K-8 focus options, including Sunnyside, where 28% of middle schoolers and 37% of middle-grade lottery students are on IEPs (Portland Public Schools, 2026). The strongest research on K8 outcomes, using statewide data from Florida and citywide data from New York City, finds that moving students to a separate middle school lowers achievement and raises absences, with the largest drops for students who were already behind (Rockoff & Lockwood, 2010; Schwerdt & West, 2013) and math losses of 0.29 standard deviations for students with disabilities, similar to their peers (O’Hagan, Stiefel & Schwartz, 2025). Evidence also suggests outdoor, place-based learning helps students with disabilities stay focused and may ease ADHD symptoms, benefits that reach neurodivergent students whether or not they have IEPs (Szczytko, Carrier & Stevenson, 2018; Hood & Baumann, 2024). ↩︎
  2. Dismantling focus options, like Sunnyside, that serve a high proportion of students with disabilities is not equitable. The focus options closing or losing grades serve 22–31% students with IEPs, compared with as few as 7% at focus options being retained under the final recommendation. ↩︎
  3. Conservative estimate: an entry-level teacher salary of $57,206 plus three paraeducators at the $24/hour required minimum wage for 990 instructional hours a year (71,280), for a total of $128,486. It excludes benefits and the other staffing a CB classroom requires. ↩︎
